\section{Algorithms and engineering}\label{sec:algorithms}

This section describes the techniques behind the engine family and says, for each, where it comes from. Three labels are used
throughout. \emph{Borrowed}: implemented from a published source, which is cited. \emph{Extended}: a published idea
that we carry to a new setting (a field, a cell type, representatives, a platform); the extension is named. \emph{New}: as far as
the project's notes and the literature we have read show; we have not carried out a systematic prior-art search, and where the
call is close the text says so. \Cref{app:catalogue} gives the full list with the same labels.

\subsection{Vietoris--Rips complexes: generation and the packed engine}\label{sec:vr}

\paragraph{In-order generation of simplices (from the preprint).}
Ripser enumerates the cofaces of a simplex by the combinatorial number system and sorts each dimension by diameter. The
preprint of Bauer, Matuszewski and Vejdemo-Johansson~\cite{bauer2024generating} shows that the simplices of one dimension can
instead be generated in filtration order from the previous dimension, without a sort: of the cofacets of a simplex that
have the same diameter as the simplex's longest edges, the lexicographically first one is determined by where the
longest edges sit, in one of three cases, and as a consequence a simplex needs only its first longest edge as metadata.
\VERIFY{statement and numbering of the preprint's Theorem~3 and Propositions~5 and~7}
\tdaj{} implements the generator (\code{LayeredInorderSimplexStream}) with four bytes of metadata per simplex, candidate
cofacets from bitset neighbourhood balls that are updated by one sweep per dimension, a mirrored vertex order so that ties come
out in Ripser's colexicographic order without further work in general position, and a check of every run of tied values that sorts the run only if it is out of order.
Generation and engine both read flat primitive arrays of values and vertices per dimension.
\PROV{whether the mirrored vertex order and the tie-run check belong to the preprint or are ours}

\paragraph{The packed Ripser engine (borrowed, extended).}
The default engine for Vietoris--Rips complexes is a port of Ripser's algorithm~\cite{bauer2021ripser}: cohomology, clearing,
implicit coboundaries through the combinatorial number system, apparent pairs, emergent pairs and the enclosing radius. What
differs is what it is generic over and what it returns. It works over any prime, not a compile-time fixed one; it returns a
representative for every bar; and it keeps its per-simplex state in primitive arrays (the level's simplices, the pairing
rows, the cleared set, hash tables keyed by index) instead of objects, so that no pair, column or bar costs an allocation
that is not needed. Three parts of this are specific enough to describe.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Index width.} A simplex is numbered by one 63-bit word, or by two when a level is too large for one
  (a combinatorial number past $2^{63}$), up to 126 bits; past that the engine asks for a lower \code{maxDimension}. The two-word
  type is separate from the one-word type, so a one-word run pays nothing for it (a cell is a plain class with a method
  for the high word, and the subclass that stores it is used only in a two-word run). The coefficient is not packed into the
  index, which is why a prime does not shrink the range of simplices the engine can number, in contrast to Ripser's coefficient
  build, which reserves eight bits of the simplex index for it.
  \VERIFY{ripser.cpp's \code{num\_coefficient\_bits} and the consequent limit $2^{55}-1$ were read in the copy shipped with ripser.py~0.6.15 (2026-10-18), not in the pinned master; and how TDA4j stores the coefficient of a column entry}
  \PROV{prior art on simplex-index width beyond 63 bits (Dory~\cite{aggarwal2021dory}, giotto-ph) has not been checked}
\item \emph{Working column.} A column is reduced on a heap over primitive arrays with lazy cancellation, as in Ripser. A
  column's reduction history is logged, and cocycles are expanded from the logs only for the bars that are reported.
\item \emph{Apparent and emergent pairs.} The test for an apparent pair decodes a column's simplex once, and finds the
  facet of smallest index from the simplex's longest edges rather than by scanning facets. An emergent pair is
  paired without being reduced, and its column is stored only as its simplex and rebuilt if a later column needs it, which
  lowers live memory at a small cost in time.
  \PROV{the rebuild-on-demand storage of emergent columns is ours; the pairs are Ripser's definition}
\end{itemize}
Under a threshold, cofacets come from per-vertex sorted neighbour lists in the order of the all-vertex scan, with bit-identical diameters; the
edge ranks that the parallel engine uses are a dense table up to a few thousand points and sorted neighbour lists above, a
choice made for memory.

\subsection{A representative for every bar}\label{sec:representatives}

Every engine returns, for every bar, a chain that witnesses it. The library's design principle, which the tests enforce, is
that nothing public drops representatives and that an optimisation which does is probably not worth having. An
engine computes one kind natively: cocycles for the cohomology engines, cycles for the homology engines and for the fast
engines. The other kind comes from the engine's own pairing by one further pass, the \emph{involution}.

\paragraph{From a cohomology pairing to cycles.}
Given the pairs of a cohomology run, only the boundary columns of the death cells are reduced, in the reverse of the order
that the cohomology run used, youngest first; the reduced column of a death cell $\tau$ is a cycle for the bar $(\sigma,\tau)$,
and its youngest cell is $\sigma$. An essential bar gets the cycle that reducing the boundary of its birth cell to zero
produces. This is the computation of \v{C}ufar and Virk~\cite{cufar2021involuted}, who give it for Vietoris--Rips
complexes, and which Ripserer.jl offers as \code{alg = :involuted}. Hang et al.~\cite{hang2021umatch} give a
related factorisation, with lazy cycle representatives, for OAT.

\paragraph{From a homology pairing to cocycles.}
Dually, from the pairing of a homology run only the coboundary columns of the birth cells are reduced, youngest first, and the
representative is the corresponding column of the reduction. The dualities of de Silva, Morozov and
Vejdemo-Johansson~\cite{desilva2011dualities} make this direction unsurprising; we have not found it stated for general
cell types.
\PROV{whether the reverse direction is in \v{C}ufar--Virk or elsewhere (the catalog checked their abstract, introduction and section outline, not every page); it is probably folklore}

\paragraph{Invariants.}
Three invariants make the pass safe, and each has caught a bug. The pairing must come from the same total order under which
the involution reduces. Zero-length pairs must be present in the pairing, since their columns are pivots that the others use,
even though the verb drops them from its output afterwards. And every pivot is checked against the paired cell: a mismatch
throws and never falls back, because a disagreement is a bug in the pairing or in the order, not a condition to recover from.
Two engines may return different, equally valid representatives for the same bar, so representatives are never compared cell
for cell across engines; they are checked for validity: a cycle (or cocycle), present at the bar's birth, killed at its death.

\paragraph{Costs and laziness.}
A cycle derived from cocycles costs a second reduction, which touches only the death columns, and an apparent pair that
nobody reports is not reduced. Representatives are \emph{lazy chains} in the packed engines: a cocycle is a node in the
reduction logs, expanded with an explicit stack when it is read (and the expansion throws if it meets a cycle in the logs,
which would be a bug). The cost of representatives is measured in \cref{sec:eval-reps}.

\paragraph{The fast engines.}
The two union-find engines (\cref{sec:dual-uf}) return cycles only and have no derived kind: their tie-breaking is by value
rather than by the stream's order, so there is no common order for an involution to reduce under. Cocycles of an image
come from the cohomology engine.

\subsection{Degree $0$ by union-find}\label{sec:h0}

Every engine that reduces degree $0$ pairs it by union-find: the edges oldest first, an edge that joins two components
kills the component with the younger root and is paired with it. The pairs are those the reduction would find, and the
representative of a bar is the indicator of the dying component (the final component, for an essential bar). The parallel
engine builds it on demand from the merge forest. The shortcut is valid only if every edge has boundary
$c\,(v-u)$ for a nonzero scalar $c$, or none, which is a property the library checks for each stream
(\code{EdgeBoundary.shape}); a Morse-reduced or hand-built complex whose edges break it falls back to column reduction.
Each engine keeps a switch (\code{useUnionFind = false}) and the reference engines always reduce by columns, so that each
specification compares the two. Union-find for degrees $0$ and $1$ is classical, and the chunk algorithm
used it already~\cite{bkr2014chunks}; what is specific here is the uniform guard and the oracles that hold it to account.
The effect on time and memory is in \cref{sec:eval-ablation}.

\subsection{Dual union-find for the top degree}\label{sec:dual-uf}

For a complex that triangulates a region of $\R^d$ in which every facet has at most two cofaces, the top homology
$H_{d-1}$ of a sublevel filtration is determined by union-find on the \emph{dual graph} of the top cells read in the
superlevel direction, with an extra vertex at infinity for the outside: a class dies when two components of the complement
merge, by Alexander duality, and the elder rule decides which. Degree $0$ is primal union-find. The two fast engines use this
for cubical images (\code{FastCubicalHomologyEngine}, with the $T$-construction, in which a cube takes the minimum of the
pixels that contain it) and for Delaunay alpha complexes (\code{FastAlphaHomologyEngine}). For $d\ge3$ the middle
degrees go to the packed cohomology engine run on the complex with its top cells hidden, which is where the degrees that
union-find cannot reach are computed; no matrix reduction is needed in $2$D.

\paragraph{Representatives.}
For a bar of degree $d-1$ the representative is the boundary of the coherently oriented sum of the top cells of the component
that dies. A signed union-find records the merge forest (parent, orientation flip, event) and freezes it; a representative is
materialised by walking the dying component's subtree only when it is read. Each merge therefore costs almost nothing and
only reported bars pay for their size. The first implementation copied the surviving component's coefficient map on every
union; replacing it with the forest turned quadratic time into near-linear (\cref{sec:eval-cubical}).
Ties need a rule, because the union-find breaks them by value rather than by the stream's order: on equal births the
smaller component dies.

\paragraph{Prior art.}
Union-find for the top Betti number of complexes in $S^3$ is due to Delfinado and Edelsbrunner~\cite{delfinado1995incremental};
duality for the persistence of images to Garin et al.~\cite{garin2020duality}; the cubical fast path in this form to Le Breton,
Szustakowski and Piraud~\cite{lebreton2026flash}, whose Flash Cubical handles the $V$-construction over $\Ftwo$ in dimensions
two and three, and whose abstract says that the ideas ``generalise naturally to $T$-filtrations''. We generalise in
four ways: to the $T$-construction, to any field, to any dimension from two, and to Delaunay complexes; and we return
representatives, which Flash Cubical does not. Our hybrid for the middle degrees hides the top cells and runs cohomology; the
relation of this to Flash Cubical's treatment of three-dimensional images is not one we have compared in detail.
\PROV{how different the middle-degree hybrid is from Flash Cubical's 3D case}

\paragraph{What the representatives are.}
Obayashi~\cite{obayashi2018volume} computes volume-optimal cycles for a generator, and Lenzen and
Renkin~\cite{lenzen2025codim1} prove that the boundaries of the components of the complement form a minimal cycle basis for
any positive weights in codimension one. Our representatives are those boundaries, and we would like to say that they are
volume-optimal at birth. We have not proved it.
\begin{conjecture}[to be proved or dropped before submission]\label{conj:volume}
For a filtration with distinct values, the representative that the dual union-find engine returns for a bar of degree $d-1$ is
the boundary of the component of the complement that is born when the class is, and so a volume-optimal cycle in the sense of
Obayashi and Lenzen--Renkin.
\end{conjecture}
\PROV{Mikael: whether to prove \cref{conj:volume} before the paper and compare with HomCloud on inputs with distinct values; ties make which bar gets which component depend on the tie-break, so representatives can legitimately differ there}
\paragraph{Preconditions.}
The alpha engine needs every facet to have at most two cofaces. The Bowyer--Watson triangulation guarantees it, and for a
Helix triangulation the engine checks it and throws \code{FastAlphaTriangulationException}; how often it fails, by ambient
dimension and point count, is measured in \cref{sec:eval-alpha}.

\subsection{Parallel reduction in units of edge blocks}\label{sec:parallel}

For a flag complex, a simplex $\sigma$ belongs to the \emph{block} of its first longest edge $e$. The blocks partition
the complex, each block is contiguous in a filtration order in general position, and a unit of work is a union of whole blocks.
The engine reduces each unit independently (phase~1), then reduces the deferred columns, those whose pivot lies in an earlier
unit, against the final pairs of earlier units in parallel (phase~2a), and reduces the remainder in order (phase~2b). The
argument for soundness is the one for chunks~\cite{bkr2014chunks}: a unit is contiguous in filtration order, so reducing it alone
and then merging gives the same pairs as one sequential reduction; blocks add that, in general position, every zero-length
pair lies inside one block. A simplex's key is its block's rank above its combinatorial index, so the diameter of a simplex is
a rank, a maximum of ranks, and the sort is on integer keys; for a very large complex the key takes two words. The pairs are
those of a sequential reduction, and the result is exact whatever the number of threads. This engine is \code{Engine.ParallelRipser}; its
representatives are lazy chains, cocycles from the logs and cycles from one parallel pass of the involution.
A given engine returns the same representatives at every thread count; but \code{Engine.Auto} chooses a different engine when
several threads are available, so with \code{Auto} the representatives can depend on the thread count, in the ties.
\PROV{Mikael: where the preprint~\cite{bauer2024generating} ends and the edge-block material begins (the preprint as arXiv serves it has no blocks, lens graphs or parallel reduction); related work on parallel reduction is Ripser++~\cite{zhang2020ripserpp}, lock-free reduction~\cite{morozov2020lockfree} and giotto-ph~\cite{burella2021giotto}}

Two observations belong with the engine. In high degrees the reduction is nearly free and the cost is enumerating cells,
which is independent from block to block: in one of our test cases a degree-ten computation needed a few hundred column
additions for tens of millions of columns. And what remains serial is a fraction that does not shrink with more threads: the
deferred columns of degree~$1$, whose reduction is a chain of dependencies; a binary tree over the units did not
shorten it. A related experiment that parallelised reduction over explicit coboundary matrices gave no speedup and is kept in
the code base only for its helpers.
\VERIFY{the quantitative claims in this paragraph come from project notes (434 additions for 41M columns, 40 points in $\R^{10}$ to $H_{10}$); re-measure before quoting}

\subsection{Alpha complexes}\label{sec:alpha}

The alpha complex has three backends and one selection rule.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Bowyer--Watson}~\cite{bowyer1981computing,watson1981computing}, in up to four affine dimensions, with exact
  predicates: a floating-point filter with an exact fallback in the style of Shewchuk~\cite{shewchuk1997adaptive}, though not adaptive,
  and a symbolic perturbation by point index in the sense of simulation of simplicity~\cite{edelsbrunner1990simulation}, so that the
  triangulation is the same for every insertion order. It is valid by construction.
\item \emph{Helix}, a frontier walk from a hull facet that works in any ambient dimension.
  \PROV{who designed the walk and which publication it follows; the scaladoc cites IEEE document 10917453, which the project's notes say could not be read, and the benchmark harness takes nothing from it}
  What we added is a validity check (every point a vertex, no facet in three top simplices, every hull facet on the hull, and
  $H_{d-1}$ of the full unfiltered complex trivial because the hull is contractible), a repair by jitter and
  re-triangulation when it fails, and parallel speculative evaluation of queued cases whose results are applied in queue order, so that
  results are identical to one thread.
\item \emph{Dual active-set quadratic programming} (DQP) of Carlsson and Carlsson~\cite{carlsson2023alpha}: no triangulation, a
  quadratic program per candidate simplex, truncated by a radius. A valid simplex of large circumradius can fail the rank
  test of the solver; \tdaj{} retries at a tighter tolerance and keeps the result only if the multipliers are certified against
  the data, because lowering the tolerance alone is unsafe (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).
\end{itemize}
Filtration values are computed top-down: a simplex takes its own circumradius when it is Gabriel with respect to its cofaces
and otherwise the minimum over them, with circumspheres by Householder reflections, never by the Gram system, which lost
digits on a sliver in four dimensions. \code{Auto} uses Bowyer--Watson up to four dimensions and Helix above, and with a radius a cost
model chooses between a triangulation and DQP; with no radius and a low degree cap in high ambient dimension it runs DQP
truncated at the enclosing radius, since a triangulation builds the whole complex whatever the radius and DQP only what lies
within it.
\VERIFY{the exact thresholds of the selection rule (ambient dimension $\ge8$ and cap $\le d-2$ at 2026-11-03, PR \#301) and whether Mikael decides to change them after the server tables}
In general position the \v{C}ech and alpha complexes have the same barcode by the persistent nerve
lemma~\cite{chazal2008persistence}, which gives an independent check of every alpha backend at dimensions where GUDHI cannot run.
Alpha complexes are cut at the enclosing radius, past which the union of balls is contractible.

\subsection{Other complexes}\label{sec:complexes}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{\v{C}ech} complexes of Euclidean points on the Vietoris--Rips coface machinery, with radii from a minimum
  enclosing ball. A computed ball can be one ulp non-monotone, so the value of a simplex is clamped to the maximum over its
  facets.
\item \textbf{Witness} complexes~\cite{desilva2004witness} on maxmin or random landmarks. The \emph{lazy} witness complex is a flag complex under a
  witness metric space, so the Ripser engines apply to it; the \emph{general} one is not a flag complex and runs on the
  generic cohomology engine.
\item \textbf{Dowker} complexes of a relation $R\subseteq L\times W$ with real values~\cite{dowker1952homology,chowdhury2018dowker}, and the
  dual, whose barcode agrees without zero-length bars. One observation from building this: the Dowker complex of the
  landmark--witness distances is the \v{C}ech complex of the landmarks on the witnesses, not the witness complex with $\nu=0$.
  \VERIFY{the observation is in the project notes (PR \#295), not checked against the literature}
\item \textbf{Distance-to-measure} weighted Rips complexes~\cite{anai2019dtm,chazal2011geometric}, with $p\in\{1,2\}$; for
  $p=1$ the barcode agrees with GUDHI's \code{DTMRipsComplex} byte for byte on the examples checked.
\item \textbf{Sparse Rips} filtrations of Cavanna, Jahanseir and Sheehy~\cite{cavanna2015sparse}, from a greedy permutation. Their
  Algorithm~3 omits a clamp that their own Section~5.3 requires (\cref{sec:references}); \tdaj{} applies the clamp to every edge.
  GUDHI implements a different sparse filtration~\cite{buchet2016efficient}, so the two $\varepsilon$ are not comparable.
\item \textbf{Edge collapse} of flag filtrations~\cite{boissonnat2020edge,glisse2022swap}: a port of GUDHI's collapser, since an independent
  redesign gave a wrong answer on a five-point example; a dense matrix form with an incremental dominator test is used for large inputs.
\item \textbf{Cubical complexes} of images in any dimension, sublevel or superlevel, with missing pixels as \code{NaN}: the
  complex is the closure of the unmasked pixels. Cubes are encoded in doubled coordinates~\cite{kaczynski2004computational}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Beyond barcodes}\label{sec:breadth}

\paragraph{Coordinates.}
Circular coordinates~\cite{desilva2011circular} come from a persistent class by harmonic smoothing with conjugate gradients, with the integer lift
checked exactly on every triangle; toroidal coordinates of Scoccola et al.~\cite{scoccola2022toroidal} decorrelate several
classes by lattice reduction (LLL on the Cholesky factor of the Gram matrix), with our own LLL; sparse variants on a landmark
Dowker complex follow Perea~\cite{perea2018multiscale}. A coordinate can start from any diagram: the cocycles are used directly or recovered, the
input must be supplied again, and a bar's class is defined only modulo the bars nested inside it.

\paragraph{Other persistence variants.}
Zigzag persistence~\cite{carlsson2010zigzag}, relative persistence (the quotient by a subcomplex as a cell type), images, kernels and cokernels
of maps between barcodes (``barcode algebra'', contributed by Nico Stucki \PROV{source of the algorithm}), discrete Morse reduction~\cite{mischaikow2013morse},
the persistent Laplacian, and exact rational coefficients, which can blow up in size and so remain opt-in.

\paragraph{Distances and vectorizations.}
Bottleneck and Wasserstein distances by Hopcroft--Karp and Hungarian matching with the conventions of Hera~\cite{kerber2017hera}
and a stated policy for essential bars, persistence landscapes~\cite{bubenik2015landscapes} and images~\cite{adams2017images}.

\paragraph{Simplicial sets and groups.}
The \code{sset} add-on gives simplicial sets with products, quotients, joins, smash products, cones and mapping cones, cup products
and Steenrod squares, fundamental groups by presentation, and nerves of groups, monoids and finite categories; the catalogue is
checked against Sage~\cite{sagemath}, with cup products as the discriminating oracle. A filtered simplicial set is a
filtered complex like any other, so persistence with representatives comes from the general engines. For persistent group
homology along a subgroup chain, Ellis and King~\cite{ellis2011persistent} introduced the notion and HAP~\cite{hap}
computes it from resolutions, which scale better than the bar construction; we compute the same barcode as an
honest filtered simplicial set, with representatives, optionally after a Brown collapse that generates the critical cells
directly~\cite{brown1992geometry,anick1986homology}. We regard this as a worked example of the constructive layer rather than as a
competitor to HAP.
